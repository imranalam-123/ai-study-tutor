# Introduction to Materials Science — Demo Study Notes

## Crystalline and amorphous solids
A crystalline solid has a long-range repeating arrangement of atoms, ions, or molecules. An amorphous solid lacks long-range periodic order, although it may still exhibit short-range order. Metals and many ceramics are crystalline, while common window glass is generally amorphous. Crystal structure and defects influence mechanical, electrical, thermal, and chemical properties.

## Unit cells and crystal structures
A unit cell is the smallest repeating volume that represents the translational symmetry of a crystal lattice. The simple cubic structure has atoms at the eight corners of a cube and contains one atom per unit cell after corner sharing is counted. The body-centered cubic (BCC) structure has corner atoms plus one atom at the body center, giving two atoms per unit cell. The face-centered cubic (FCC) structure has corner atoms and atoms at the center of each face, giving four atoms per unit cell.

The coordination number describes the number of nearest-neighbor atoms. For ideal simple cubic, BCC, and FCC structures, the coordination numbers are 6, 8, and 12 respectively. The atomic packing factor is the fraction of a unit-cell volume occupied by hard-sphere atoms. BCC has an ideal atomic packing factor of about 0.68, while FCC has about 0.74. FCC is therefore more closely packed than BCC in the hard-sphere model.

## Mechanical properties
Stress is force per unit area, and engineering strain is the change in length divided by the original length. In the elastic region, stress is approximately proportional to strain for many metals; the slope of the linear stress-strain curve is Young's modulus. Elastic deformation is largely recoverable after removing the load. Plastic deformation is permanent and involves mechanisms such as dislocation motion.

Yield strength describes the stress at which substantial plastic deformation begins, using a defined criterion such as the 0.2 percent offset method. Ultimate tensile strength is the maximum engineering stress reached during a tensile test. Ductility describes the capacity to plastically deform before fracture and is often reported using elongation or reduction in area. Hardness measures resistance to localized plastic deformation and depends on the test method and material condition.

## Dislocations and strengthening
A dislocation is a line defect in a crystal lattice. Plastic deformation in many metals occurs primarily through dislocation motion rather than through all atoms sliding simultaneously. Grain boundaries, solute atoms, precipitates, and other dislocations can impede dislocation motion and increase strength. Grain refinement often increases yield strength, a trend described by the Hall-Petch relationship for many polycrystalline materials over an applicable grain-size range.

Solid-solution strengthening occurs when dissolved solute atoms distort the lattice and interact with dislocations. Precipitation strengthening uses fine particles that obstruct dislocation motion. Work hardening increases dislocation density during plastic deformation, making further motion more difficult. These strengthening mechanisms can trade off against ductility, toughness, conductivity, or other properties depending on the system.

## Heat treatment of steel
Steel is primarily an iron-carbon alloy, and its properties depend on composition, processing, and microstructure. Austenitizing heats steel into a temperature range where austenite forms, subject to composition and the relevant phase boundaries. Quenching cools the steel rapidly and may form hard, brittle martensite when the composition and cooling conditions permit. Tempering reheats quenched steel below the eutectoid temperature to reduce brittleness and adjust strength and toughness.

Annealing commonly involves heating and controlled cooling to soften material, improve ductility, or reduce residual stress, depending on the specific treatment. Normalizing usually involves austenitizing steel followed by cooling in still air; it often creates a finer structure than slow furnace cooling. Actual heat-treatment schedules must be selected for the alloy grade, component size, required properties, and applicable safety procedures.

## Corrosion basics
Corrosion is the degradation of a material through chemical or electrochemical interaction with its environment. In aqueous corrosion of steel, anodic dissolution of iron may be coupled with a cathodic reaction such as oxygen reduction in neutral aerated water. Corrosion rate is influenced by electrolyte chemistry, temperature, oxygen availability, potential, microstructure, and protective films.

Common corrosion-control strategies include selecting suitable materials, applying coatings, controlling the environment, using inhibitors where appropriate, and designing cathodic protection systems. A coating that is damaged may expose a small anodic region coupled to a large cathodic region, which can accelerate localized attack under some conditions. Inspection and maintenance are necessary because corrosion protection is not always permanent.
